Advanced risk-transfer contracts

Derivatives

A derivative gets its value from an underlying share, index, currency, interest rate, commodity or another permitted reference. It can hedge risk—but leverage can multiply losses quickly.

Start here

A contract, not ownership of the underlying

Complex and leveraged

Exchange-traded derivatives are standardised contracts cleared under exchange rules. They are primarily used for hedging, speculation and arbitrage. Their expiry, lot size, strike, settlement method and margin requirements are contract-specific.

Hedging

Take an offsetting position intended to reduce an existing price exposure. A hedge can be imperfect and has costs.

Speculation

Take directional or volatility exposure without owning the underlying. Leverage can create losses larger than the initial margin.

Arbitrage

Attempt to capture related-market price differences. Execution, funding, transaction costs and timing can remove the apparent profit.

SEBI warning: derivatives are zero-sum before costs, and official studies have found that the large majority of individual equity F&O traders incurred net losses. Beginners should learn simpler products first.

Contract types

Futures, options, forwards and swaps

Rights and obligations differ
Futures

Standardised exchange contracts to buy or sell an underlying at an agreed price on a future date. Both long and short sides have obligations and post margin.

Call option

Gives the buyer a right—but not an obligation—to buy or receive the contract’s specified payoff. The buyer pays a premium; the seller accepts the corresponding obligation.

Put option

Gives the buyer a right—but not an obligation—to sell or receive the specified downside payoff. It can be used for protection or directional trading.

Forwards

Privately negotiated over-the-counter contracts. Custom terms can help hedging, but liquidity, documentation and counterparty exposure differ from exchange futures.

Swaps

Agreements to exchange cash-flow streams, such as fixed versus floating interest or one currency exposure for another. Primarily institutional and contract-specific.

Underlying markets

Equity indices, individual securities, currencies, interest-rate products and commodities may have permitted derivative contracts.

Mechanics

Lot size, margin, premium and settlement

Small cash outlay, large exposure
Contract value

Quoted price multiplied by the exchange-defined lot size. This—not merely the margin or premium—shows the economic exposure.

Futures margin

Collateral collected against potential loss. Positions are marked to market, and adverse moves can require more funds or trigger broker square-off.

Option premium

The buyer pays premium upfront. The buyer’s loss is generally limited to premium, while an uncovered option seller can face very large or theoretically unlimited loss.

Expiry

Contracts end on a defined date. Depending on product and rules, settlement can be cash-based or involve delivery.

Open interest

The number of outstanding contracts. It is not the same as trading volume and does not prove that exit will be easy at a fair price.

Charges

Brokerage, exchange charges, taxes, stamp duty and bid–ask spread reduce results. Turnover can be much larger than funds deposited.

Trading process

How an exchange-traded derivative order works

Use only risk capital
  1. Open a trading account with a SEBI-registered broker. Complete KYC and the broker’s derivatives-segment activation, income proof or suitability steps where required.
  2. Read the contract specification. Confirm underlying, exchange, expiry, lot size, strike, option type, tick size, settlement and delivery consequences.
  3. Calculate maximum plausible loss. For futures and short options, do not confuse required margin with maximum loss. Plan position size and a cash buffer.
  4. Choose the exact order. Select buy or sell, contract, quantity and order type. A limit order controls price but may not execute; a market order controls immediacy but not execution price.
  5. Monitor margin and expiry. Track mark-to-market debits, margin changes, liquidity and broker cut-off rules. Close, roll or prepare for settlement before deadlines.
  6. Reconcile records. Check the exchange contract note, ledger, margin statement, charges and realised profit or loss.

Hypothetical futures example

An index future is quoted at 20,000 with a fictional lot size of 50. Contract exposure is 20,000 × 50 = ₹10,00,000.

If the trader buys one lot and the futures price rises to 20,200, gross profit is (20,200 − 20,000) × 50 = ₹10,000.

If it falls to 19,600, gross loss is (20,000 − 19,600) × 50 = ₹20,000. The margin deposited may be far below ₹10 lakh, but profit and loss use the full contract exposure.

Hypothetical call-option example

A call has strike ₹100, premium ₹5 and fictional lot size 1,000. Buyer’s premium outlay is ₹5,000.

If expiry settlement value is ₹112, intrinsic value is ₹12,000 and gross payoff after premium is ₹7,000, before costs and tax.

If expiry value is ₹100 or below, the option can expire worthless and the buyer loses the ₹5,000 premium. The seller has the opposite payoff and potentially much larger risk.

Examples are educational: contract sizes, prices, margins, exercise and settlement rules are fictional. Never use them to place a live order.

Risk controls

What can cause losses?

Loss can exceed initial funds
Leverage

A small underlying move can cause a large percentage gain or loss relative to margin or premium paid.

Gap and volatility risk

Prices can jump beyond a planned exit level. Option prices also move with implied volatility and time decay.

Margin shortfall

Exchanges or brokers can increase requirements. Failure to add funds can lead to forced closure at an unfavourable price.

Liquidity

Distant expiries or strikes can have wide spreads. A displayed price does not guarantee execution for the desired quantity.

Expiry and delivery

Ignoring expiry may produce cash settlement, delivery obligations, auction or penalties depending on the contract.

Operational fraud

Do not share credentials or trade through unregistered tip providers, fake apps or informal “dabba” arrangements.

Tax and records

Trading results require proper accounting

Trader-specific treatment

Exchange-traded F&O activity is commonly evaluated under business-income provisions, with turnover, expenses, loss set-off, record-keeping and audit questions depending on the facts and applicable tax year. Commodity and overseas contracts can differ. Preserve contract notes, ledgers, margin statements and bank records, and obtain professional advice.
