Physical goods and market exposure

Commodities

Commodity exposure can come from physical ownership, exchange-traded funds or permitted derivatives. Each route has different custody, tracking, leverage, liquidity and tax consequences.

Commodity groups

Metals, energy and agriculture behave differently

Global and local price drivers
Precious metals

Gold and silver respond to currency, interest rates, inflation expectations, risk sentiment, industrial demand and global flows.

Base metals

Copper, aluminium, zinc, lead and nickel are sensitive to manufacturing, construction, inventories, energy costs and global trade.

Energy

Crude oil and natural gas react to supply policy, geopolitics, storage, weather, transport constraints and demand cycles.

Agriculture

Crop prices depend on acreage, weather, yield, seasonality, storage, quality, imports, exports and Government policy.

Commodity indices

An index combines multiple commodity contracts under stated weights and roll rules, reducing single-commodity concentration but adding methodology risk.

Currency effect

Indian prices can move because of both the global commodity price and the rupee exchange rate, plus domestic taxes and basis differences.

Ways to participate

Choose the exposure before choosing the product

Derivatives are not simple investments
Physical commodity

Direct ownership creates purity, storage, insurance, transport, wastage and resale-spread issues. It may suit consumption better than short-term trading.

Commodity ETF or fund

Eligible exchange-traded or mutual-fund products can offer exposure without personal storage. Review NAV tracking, expenses, liquidity and what the scheme actually holds.

Compare gold routes →
Commodity futures

Leveraged contracts with exchange-defined quantity, quality, delivery location, expiry and settlement. Daily margining applies.

Understand futures mechanics →
Commodity options

The buyer pays premium for a defined right; the seller takes the corresponding obligation and margin exposure. Exercise and settlement depend on contract terms.

Producer or user hedge

A farmer, processor, jeweller, manufacturer or importer may use derivatives to reduce adverse price risk in a genuine commercial exposure.

Commodity-linked companies

Mining, energy or agriculture-related shares are businesses, not direct commodity holdings. Management, costs, debt and regulation can dominate returns.

Contract specification

Quantity and quality are part of the trade

Delivery obligations can apply
Lot and quotation

Know whether the quote is per gram, kilogram, barrel, tonne or another unit, then multiply by lot quantity to find contract exposure.

Quality grade

Deliverable commodities must satisfy exchange specifications. Grade, assay, moisture, location and premium or discount rules can affect settlement.

Expiry and delivery

Some contracts move into compulsory delivery or tender periods. Broker square-off deadlines can be earlier than exchange deadlines.

Basis

The difference between local spot price and futures price can change. A hedge may not perfectly offset because location, grade or timing differs.

Contango and backwardation

Later futures can trade above or below nearby contracts. Rolling repeated futures exposure can create a gain or cost independent of spot movement.

Daily price limits

Exchange limits and low liquidity can prevent immediate exit even while losses or delivery obligations remain.

Trading process

How to trade commodity futures or options

Verify delivery before ordering
  1. Use a SEBI-registered broker with commodity access. Complete KYC and activate the relevant exchange segment.
  2. Select the commodity and contract. Check exchange, expiry, lot, quote unit, quality, delivery centre, settlement and tender-period rules.
  3. Calculate total exposure. Multiply quote by lot quantity. Treat margin as collateral—not the purchase price and not the maximum loss.
  4. Choose hedge or trade direction. A genuine buyer may go long to offset a future purchase-price rise; a producer may go short against future sale-price risk.
  5. Place and monitor the order. Use a suitable order type, keep excess funds for mark-to-market and watch volatility, open interest and spread.
  6. Exit or prepare for settlement. Close or roll before the broker’s cut-off unless you understand and can meet every delivery requirement.

Hypothetical commodity-futures trade

A fictional metal future is quoted at ₹800 per unit with a lot size of 1,000 units. Contract exposure is ₹8,00,000.

A trader buys one lot. If the quote rises to ₹812, gross profit is (₹812 − ₹800) × 1,000 = ₹12,000.

If it falls to ₹775, gross loss is (₹800 − ₹775) × 1,000 = ₹25,000. Margin deposited may be much smaller than ₹8 lakh, but the full lot drives profit and loss.

Hypothetical business hedge

A manufacturer expects to buy 1,000 units of a metal in three months and buys a matching futures contract at ₹800. If spot and futures both rise by about ₹20, the physical purchase costs roughly ₹20,000 more while the futures may gain roughly ₹20,000.

If prices fall, the cheaper physical purchase can be offset by a futures loss. The objective is price stability—not extra profit. Basis, quantity, timing and costs prevent a perfect hedge.

Examples are fictional: real contracts have different lot sizes, prices, margins, taxes and settlement rules. Always read the current exchange contract specification.

Risks

Commodity exposure has more than price risk

Capital loss and delivery risk
Leverage and margin

Losses can exceed initial margin, and requirements can rise suddenly during volatile markets.

Weather and policy

Agricultural output, import or export rules, stock limits and Government intervention can move prices abruptly.

Geopolitics

Sanctions, wars, shipping disruption and producer policy can gap energy and metal prices.

Storage and delivery

Physical settlement brings warehouse, quality, transport, tax and documentation responsibilities.

Liquidity and basis

A specific expiry or grade can trade thinly. The derivative may not track the user’s local cash price precisely.

Product mismatch

An ETF, futures contract, commodity-company share and physical commodity do not produce the same return.

Safer process

Checks before participating

Regulated route only
  1. Define the purpose. Diversification, physical use and hedging need different products.
  2. Verify registration. Trade only through recognised exchanges and SEBI-registered intermediaries; reject assured-return and informal dabba-trading offers.
  3. Read every specification. Especially delivery, quality, tender period, position limits and settlement.
  4. Limit exposure. Maintain a separate emergency fund and never assume stop-loss orders eliminate gap risk.
  5. Keep records. Preserve contract notes, ledgers, margin statements, delivery documents and expense records for reconciliation and tax.

Tax caution: physical holdings, ETFs or funds, commodity derivatives and commodity-company shares can receive different tax and accounting treatment. Confirm the applicable year’s rules with a qualified professional.
