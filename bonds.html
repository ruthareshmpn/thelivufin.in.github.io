Lending through tradable instruments

Debt Securities

Debt securities represent money lent by investors to a Government, company, municipality or other eligible issuer. The instrument defines the interest or discount, repayment date, security, priority and investor rights.

Core idea

You are a lender, not an owner

Terms determine the outcome

When you buy a debt security, the issuer owes amounts described in its legal documents. A conventional bond may pay a coupon and return face value at maturity; a zero-coupon instrument is issued below face value; a floating-rate issue resets its coupon using a stated benchmark.

Face value

The contractual principal amount used for coupon calculation and maturity repayment. It can differ from the market purchase price.

Coupon

The stated interest rate or payment formula. A high coupon can reflect higher issuer, structure or liquidity risk.

Yield

The return implied by the market price, coupon, time to maturity and repayment assumptions. Coupon and yield are not interchangeable.

Maturity

The scheduled repayment date. Callable, puttable, perpetual or amortising securities require additional cash-flow analysis.

Security

Secured debt has identified collateral or charge, but recovery value and enforcement time are uncertain. Unsecured debt relies primarily on issuer credit.

Seniority

Payment priority matters during distress. Senior debt generally ranks before subordinated debt, while equity ranks behind creditors.

Corporate and institutional debt

Read the issuer and the instrument together

Credit loss is possible
Issuer capacity

Review operating cash flow, leverage, interest coverage, refinancing needs, contingent liabilities and group-company exposure.

Credit rating

A rating is an opinion, not a guarantee. Check the rating rationale, outlook, watch status and subsequent revisions from every appointed agency.

Offer documents

Read use of proceeds, security cover, covenants, events of default, trustee terms, related-party exposure and redemption schedule.

Secured versus unsecured

Collateral can improve recovery prospects but does not assure full or timely payment. Confirm charge ranking and asset coverage.

Call and put options

A call can end a high-coupon investment early; a put may provide an exit only on specified dates and conditions.

Liquidity

An exchange listing does not ensure an active buyer. Check trading frequency, bid–ask spread and realistic exit price before purchase.

Price and return

Coupon, current yield and yield to maturity

Market value can change
Interest-rate risk

When market yields rise, prices of existing fixed-rate debt generally fall. Longer duration normally means greater price sensitivity.

Credit-spread risk

A weaker credit view can push the required yield higher and market price lower even when benchmark rates do not move.

Reinvestment risk

Coupons or early redemption proceeds may need to be reinvested at a lower rate than the original security.

Simple price-and-yield example

A ₹1,000 face-value bond with an 8% annual coupon pays ₹80 a year. If purchased at ₹950, its current yield is about 8.42% (₹80 ÷ ₹950).

If held to a ₹1,000 maturity repayment, the investor may also receive a ₹50 price gain. Yield to maturity accounts for coupon timing, purchase price, maturity value and time—not merely the coupon.

Buying and holding

Primary issue, exchange or regulated platform

Verify the channel
  1. Choose the category. Match issuer type, maturity, cash-flow dates and risk to the goal.
  2. Read the official document. Use the prospectus, information memorandum, term sheet, exchange filing and current rating rationale.
  3. Verify the platform. Government securities can be accessed through RBI Retail Direct and permitted exchange channels. For online bond platforms, check the current SEBI list of providers registered with NSE or BSE.
  4. Compare using yield, not coupon alone. Include accrued interest, clean or dirty price, taxes, brokerage and other charges.
  5. Plan the exit. Holding to maturity still carries issuer risk; selling early adds market-price and liquidity risk.

Tax and exit

Income, sale and redemption can be treated differently

Check the tax year
Interest or coupon

Interest is generally taxable according to the investor’s applicable provisions and rate. TDS, if any, does not necessarily equal the final tax liability.

Sale before maturity

A secondary-market sale can create a capital gain or loss. Holding period, listing, instrument type and current law determine the treatment.

Redemption

The difference between purchase cost and redemption proceeds can require instrument-specific tax analysis, especially for zero-coupon, discounted or market-linked structures.

No generic preclosure

A debt security is not an FD. Exit may require an exchange sale, an issuer call or put, tender offer, or holding until maturity.

Loan against securities

A lender may accept eligible debt securities as collateral, applying its own valuation haircut, interest rate and margin calls. Availability is not a right attached to every security.

Default or restructuring

Missed payments, resolution or restructuring can delay recovery and create legal and tax consequences. Security and ratings do not eliminate loss.

Tax caution: debt-security taxation has changed repeatedly and differs by instrument and transaction. Confirm current Income Tax provisions or obtain professional advice before relying on an after-tax yield.
