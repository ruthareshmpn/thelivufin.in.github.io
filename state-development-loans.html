State Government fixed income

State Development Loans (SDLs)

SDLs are dated Government securities through which Indian State Governments and Union Territories borrow from the market. They normally pay a fixed coupon every six months and repay face value on the maturity date.

Core idea

You lend to the issuing State Government

Government security

RBI manages SDL auctions in consultation with the respective State Governments. Each SDL belongs to a specific issuer and maturity. The State Government is responsible for its payments; an SDL is not a Central Government security or a bank deposit.

Issuer

A named State Government or eligible Union Territory. Read the auction notification for the exact issuer, maturity and terms.

Coupon

Usually a fixed annual percentage paid in two half-yearly instalments. A new security's coupon can be determined through a yield auction.

Maturity value

Face value is scheduled to be repaid on maturity. The purchase price may be above or below face value, so coupon and yield can differ.

Market price

Value changes with interest rates, remaining maturity, demand and liquidity. Selling before maturity can create a gain or loss.

Holding route

Retail investors can hold eligible SDLs in an RBI Retail Direct Gilt account or through another permitted intermediary.

Not DICGC insurance

SDLs are Government securities, not bank deposits. DICGC deposit insurance does not apply.

Return and risk

Government security does not mean a fixed market value

Price and liquidity risk
Interest-rate risk

If market yields rise after purchase, the SDL price generally falls. Longer-duration securities normally react more strongly.

Liquidity risk

Some SDLs trade less frequently than Central Government securities. A quick sale may require accepting a wider spread or lower price.

Reinvestment risk

Each half-yearly coupon may have to be reinvested at a lower prevailing rate.

State-specific exposure

The repayment obligation belongs to the issuing State or Union Territory. Do not treat every SDL as identical.

Inflation risk

A fixed coupon may lose purchasing power if inflation exceeds the investment's after-tax return.

Operational risk

Choosing the wrong ISIN or misunderstanding accrued interest can change the transaction outcome.

Before buying: compare yield to maturity, maturity date, coupon dates, traded volume and issuer—not the coupon alone.

How to invest

Buy in an auction or in the secondary market

RBI Retail Direct available
  1. Open an RBI Retail Direct Gilt account. Eligible individuals need an Indian rupee savings account, PAN, a valid KYC document, email address and registered mobile number.
  2. Choose primary or secondary market. In a primary auction, retail investors can place a non-competitive bid without selecting a yield. In the secondary market, choose an already-issued SDL and place an order at the available price.
  3. Check the security. Confirm the issuer, ISIN, coupon, maturity date, clean price, accrued interest and total payable amount.
  4. Fund the order. Auction funding initially uses an indicative price, accrued interest and refundable markup. Final allotment is at the weighted-average price of successful competitive bids.
  5. Review the allotment or trade. Check face value credited, final price, coupon calendar and settlement statement.
  6. Plan the holding period. Hold to maturity for scheduled face-value repayment, or sell earlier at the available market price.

Worked example

Coupon cash flow and effective yield are different

Illustrative numbers

Example: ₹1,00,000 face value with a 7.20% coupon

Half-yearly coupon: ₹1,00,000 × 7.20% ÷ 2 = ₹3,600

Two coupon payments in a full year: ₹7,200

Scheduled face-value repayment at maturity: ₹1,00,000

If purchased at face value, the simple annual coupon rate is 7.20%, before tax.

If bought for ₹98,000 plus accrued interest, the yield is higher than 7.20% because the investor pays below face value. If bought above ₹1,00,000, the yield is lower. Use yield to maturity—not coupon alone—for comparison.

Illustration only: this is not a current SDL offer or rate. Check live securities, auction calendars and prices on official or permitted platforms.

Early exit and collateral

There is no FD-style premature closure

Market exit
Sell before maturity

Place a secondary-market sell order through RBI Retail Direct or another permitted channel. Proceeds depend on the executable price and accrued interest.

No fixed penalty

An ordinary market sale has no deposit preclosure penalty. A price loss, spread and transaction charges can still reduce proceeds.

State buyback

A State may separately repurchase securities through an announced process. Do not assume a buyback will be available when cash is needed.

Loan against an SDL

A lender may accept Government securities under a pledge, lien or collateral arrangement, but a retail loan is not automatically included.

Lender terms

Ask about eligible ISINs, valuation haircut, loan-to-value, interest, margin calls and treatment of coupon or maturity proceeds.

Practical comparison

Compare the loan's total cost with the price and tax impact of selling. Obtain written terms before depending on this route.

Tax and records

Coupon income and sale gains need separate review

Taxpayer-specific
Coupon income

For an investor who does not hold the SDL as trading inventory, coupon interest is generally taxable at the applicable rate under relevant income-tax provisions.

Sale before maturity

A secondary-market sale can create a capital gain or loss. Treatment depends on current law, holding period, listing and investor facts.

Accrued interest

A transaction between coupon dates can include accrued interest. Preserve statements so income and capital components can be reviewed correctly.

Useful records

Keep the auction allotment, ISIN, prices, accrued interest, coupon credits, maturity proceeds and charges.

Tax caution: rules can change. Confirm the treatment for the relevant financial year, especially for business income, non-resident status or a sale near a coupon date.

Quick comparison

SDLs versus nearby fixed-income choices

Match the product to the goal
Central G-Sec

Both are dated Government securities, but the issuer differs. SDLs can offer a different yield and may have lower secondary-market liquidity.

T-Bill

SDLs are generally longer-dated coupon securities. T-Bills are short-term Central Government instruments issued at a discount.

Bank FD

An FD is a bank deposit with withdrawal rules and eligible DICGC coverage limits. An SDL is a tradeable security whose price changes.

Corporate bond

A corporate bond depends on company credit and legal terms. An SDL is issued by a State Government or eligible Union Territory.
